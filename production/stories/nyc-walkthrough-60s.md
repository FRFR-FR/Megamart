# "NYC: The City That Never Walks in a Straight Line"
Format: 60-second vertical NYC story short, one steady British male narrator, front-facing crowd opening followed by wide, scene-specific city coverage; keep people prominent while revealing the real city environment.
Facts: Manhattan's 1811 Commissioners' Plan established a regular street grid but preserved Broadway's older diagonal route; Times Square grew around Broadway and Seventh Avenue. (NYC Municipal Archives, Randel's 1811 Manhattan street map; NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Broadway and the Manhattan street grid)

Manhattan's street grid was drawn in eighteen eleven, but Broadway still cuts across it.
Tourist Mia steps onto a Midtown sidewalk that seems to go on forever.
Yellow taxis sweep past, buses rumble, delivery bikes weave through crowds, and skyscrapers rise like cliffs above them.
Then Times Square opens up, blazing with giant screens, theatre signs, and performers.
Mia is barely a speck beneath the billboards.
She turns north past Rockefeller Center and St Patrick's towering stone spires.
Grand Central appears ahead, its huge entrance framed by traffic and commuters.
Wait, how did one crooked road create so many famous city spaces?
You see, the Commissioners' Plan of eighteen eleven laid a regular grid of numbered streets and avenues across Manhattan, but kept Broadway's older winding route.
Ever drawn neat parallel lines, then dragged a ruler diagonally across the page?
That diagonal shaped famous meeting places like Times Square and Herald Square.
At Grand Central, stone columns frame commuters rushing through its giant entrance.
Fifth Avenue carries Mia past the Empire State Building's soaring crown.
The walk ends in Central Park, where shaded paths lead to Bethesda Fountain.
Along the way, food carts steam, horns echo off glass, and crowds cross in every direction.
The traffic fades, but the skyline still towers beyond the trees.
And Broadway? Still refusing to walk in a straight line.
